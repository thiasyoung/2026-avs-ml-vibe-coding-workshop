SELF-CONSISTENCY

Use self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023, "Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models," ICLR 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171) at the key decision points of this workflow. In that method, instead of committing to a single chain of reasoning, a model samples several diverse reasoning paths to the same question and selects the final answer that the paths most consistently agree on. Agreement across independent paths is evidence of a reliable answer; disagreement signals uncertainty.

Adapt it to this workflow as follows:

1. Decision points. Apply it at least to these decisions: (a) the cleaning and duplicate-measurement policy (Step 5), (b) the train/validation/test split design (Step 5), (c) the molecular representation (Step 5), (d) the candidate algorithms (Step 6), (e) the focused improvement to try (Step 9), and (f) the final interpretation of whether the goal was met (Step 10).
2. Diverse, independent paths. For each decision, write three short reasoning paths that each reach a concrete, comparable answer (for example, "Morgan count fingerprint radius 2 plus ~15 RDKit descriptors"). Make the paths genuinely different in their reasoning, not paraphrases: for example, one from physical-chemistry reasoning about intermolecular forces, one from statistical/ML reasoning about dataset size and bias-variance, and one from a skeptical reviewer looking for leakage or failure modes. Write each path to its conclusion before reading the others, and do not let an earlier path's answer anchor a later one.
3. Aggregate by consistency. Reduce each path's answer to comparable options and choose the option most paths support. Record the agreement (for example, 3/3 or 2/3).
4. Treat disagreement as information. When the paths split, do not settle it by eloquence. If the disagreement is empirical and affordable to test, carry the competing options into Step 7 as a controlled validation experiment and let validation evidence decide. If it concerns validity (leakage, units, test-set use), choose the more conservative option.
5. Keep it bounded and honest. The paths are short (a few sentences each), not essays. Explain in the notebook that you are generating these paths within a single session, so they approximate, rather than reproduce, the independently sampled paths of the paper; empirical validation is what makes the final choice trustworthy.
6. Make it visible. For each decision point, show a compact markdown table (path, reasoning summary, answer) followed by the chosen answer, the agreement level, and how any disagreement was resolved. Save all decisions to a self_consistency_log.csv artifact, and in Step 10 briefly reflect on which decisions had low agreement and whether validation evidence confirmed or overturned the majority choice.

Self-consistency also has a natural modeling analog: agreement among an ensemble of models or random seeds. If you use it as an applicability or uncertainty signal, describe it as a diagnostic of model disagreement, not a calibrated probability or guaranteed error bound.

THE TEN STEPS

Steps 0-3 are short because the goal, inputs/outputs, and data are already defined. Devote the implementation and teaching detail to Steps 4-10.

Step 0: Conditioning the Coding Assistant

Step 1: Set Goal
State the goal: predict reported boiling point in kelvin from SMILES. Use validation MAE in kelvin as the selection metric, with RMSE and R-squared as supporting metrics. Before fitting, set a transparent workshop success criterion, such as a substantial held-out MAE improvement over a training-median baseline; do not invent an industrial accuracy requirement or promise that it will be met. Do not claim verified normal boiling points at a fixed pressure unless the provenance establishes that condition.

Step 2: Define Inputs and Outputs
Input: one SMILES string. Output: a numerical BP prediction in K (Celsius may be shown with explicit conversion) plus an applicability status. State how invalid, unsupported, and unfamiliar molecules will be handled. No target value or external lookup may be needed at prediction time.

Step 3: Collect Data

Step 4: Examine Data
Load and preview the data. Audit the schema, missing or nonfinite targets, invalid or empty SMILES, duplicate rows, equivalent molecular structures (canonical identity), conflicting repeated measurements, mixtures, salts, charges, isotopes, stereochemistry, and implausible temperatures. Report counts and representative flagged examples with reasons; a suspicious observation is not automatically an error.

Separate deterministic validity and identity checks from target-informed exploration. After the minimal checks needed to define eligible molecules, reserve the data splits before any target-informed exploration or learned preprocessing, and use training data only for the plots and investigations that guide modeling. Include one or two readable data figures with units and sample counts.

Step 5: Data Carpentry
Using self-consistency, decide and implement a conservative cleaning policy with an audit of every exclusion and change. Preserve original SMILES and row identifiers for traceability. Canonicalize molecular identity consistently, keep equivalent structures in one split, handle repeated measurements by a documented rule, and do not merge chemically distinct forms or drop difficult examples because they hurt performance. Define the supported chemistry and apply the same policy at inference.

Using self-consistency, choose and freeze the split design. An 80/10/10 split of unique molecules with seed 42 is a reasonable default; a scaffold- or similarity-grouped split is also defensible if justified (handle acyclic molecules sensibly). Explain what the chosen split estimates and its limits for unfamiliar chemical families. Save assignments and assert disjointness. Never create new splits later to improve a score.

Using self-consistency, engineer a compact, chemically motivated representation from SMILES. Consider what drives boiling point (molecular size and polarizability, shape and branching, hydrogen bonding, polarity, aromaticity, halogens) and how descriptors, fingerprints, or a graph representation could capture it. Present the chemistry as hypotheses, not guaranteed laws. Fit imputation, scaling, and feature selection on training data only (within each fold if cross-validation is used). Step 6: Algorithm

Step 7: Experiment
